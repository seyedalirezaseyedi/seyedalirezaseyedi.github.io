01 · Decision Context

Managing a renewable resource when extraction and ecological value evolve together

Forest-management decisions affect more than current harvest volume. Biomass, biodiversity and cumulative harvesting pressure evolve jointly over time, so short-run extraction choices can alter future ecological productivity, ecological value and the feasible management path.

Economic dimension

Harvesting creates current economic value, while standing biomass has a dynamic shadow value that changes under alternative governance structures.

Ecological dimension

Biodiversity contributes directly to biomass productivity, while cumulative harvesting pressure creates an ecological-memory channel linking past extraction to future biodiversity dynamics.

02 · Challenge

How can competing economic and environmental objectives be compared dynamically?

The analytical challenge was to compare how decentralized and coordinated decision structures change harvesting paths, ecological stocks and welfare when management choices have persistent effects over a finite horizon.

Core analytical challenge: How do alternative governance regimes change the dynamic balance among biomass, biodiversity, harvesting pressure, ecological value and economic welfare?
03 · Model

A finite-horizon bioeconomic system with ecological feedback and memory

BiomassA renewable economic and ecological stock whose growth is influenced by biodiversity and harvesting.
BiodiversityAn endogenous ecological state that contributes to biomass productivity and is affected by cumulative harvesting pressure.
Cumulative pressureAn ecological-memory state transmitting the effects of past extraction into future biodiversity dynamics.
Disturbance exposure is represented in the dashboard implementation as a deterministic expected biodiversity-loss drift rather than realized stochastic shocks.

Numerical analysis

Scenario comparison

Sensitivity analysis

08 · Results

Evidence-backed differences in ecological trajectories, incentives and adjustment paths

Governance changes the dynamic pathThe published model and dashboard show distinct biomass, biodiversity, cumulative-pressure and harvest trajectories under decentralized and cooperative regimes.
Endpoint design mattersFixed ecological targets meet prescribed terminal states but can require stronger late-horizon adjustments.
Adaptive cooperation changes recoveryIn the published baseline, free-terminal cooperative regulation produces smoother adjustment and the strongest biodiversity recovery among the compared regimes.
Shadow values reveal incentive differencesA positive biomass shadow-value wedge identifies a model-implied gap between cooperative and decentralized marginal valuation of standing biomass.
Sensitivity exposes structural driversRe-solved parameter scenarios show how changes in ecological damage, biodiversity regeneration, discounting, pressure decay, biomass price and disturbance-loss drift propagate through the system.
Objectives require careful interpretationNoncooperative and cooperative objective values use different definitions and should not be read as directly comparable market revenues.
Evidence boundary: These are model-based comparative results. They do not demonstrate real-world policy adoption, realized conservation gains, or commercial financial returns.
09 · Decision Relevance

Why This Matters for Environmental Decision Science

The value of the framework is its ability to make dynamic trade-offs visible before a management rule is chosen. It shows how extraction incentives, ecological feedback, endpoint design and parameter uncertainty can be compared within a consistent quantitative system.

Resource-allocation strategy

Compare how decentralized and coordinated extraction rules propagate through renewable-resource dynamics.

Governance design

Evaluate the consequences of fixed ecological targets versus more adaptive terminal conditions.

Incentive diagnostics

Use shadow-value differences to identify where decentralized incentives diverge from cooperative valuation.

Robustness analysis

Test how ecological and economic parameter changes alter trajectories and endpoint outcomes before applying the framework to a calibrated real-world setting.

A real forest-management application would require external calibration of timber prices, harvest costs, biodiversity indices, disturbance exposure, ecological parameters, monitoring quality and enforcement constraints for the target forest.
